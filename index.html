The marathon record is faster than every Boston finisher

I took the whole 2014 Boston Marathon field, fitted the distribution its finish times form, and marked Kelvin Kiptum's 2:00:35 on the same axis. The race winner ran 2:09. The median runner took 3:52, which is 93% longer than the record.

2.4 SD
The record sits 2.4 standard deviations below the field mean of 4:03 (SD 51 minutes).

A shifted log-normal fits the body of the field: right-skewed, peaking around 3:30 to 3:50, with a long slow tail.

Fitted, the field is log-normal, with a median of 3:52 and a slow tail that pulls the mean to 4:03. Kiptum's record lies to the left of the whole curve, winner included.

The fast edge has a limit

The body fit says little about the extreme fast end, so the tail gets its own model. I took the fastest 5% of finishers (1,593 runners under 2:56) and fitted a generalized Pareto distribution to how far each one beat that threshold. The shape parameter came out at ξ = −0.147, and a negative shape means the tail ends at a finite point. Because the method is unreliable at locating that point, I don't quote a fastest possible time.

What this doesn't show

Boston is a qualifying race, so everyone in this field was already fast enough to enter. Every percentile on this page is about this one field; a general-entry marathon would sit slower and push the record further out.

The record on this page is Kelvin Kiptum's 2:00:35 (Chicago, 2023), the value hard-coded in analyze.py. A faster record would move the left mark and none of the field statistics.

I don't quote the record's rarity as "1 in N". That would mean trusting the fitted density far beyond the data, so the record is described by what was observed: faster than all 31,842 finishers, 2.4 standard deviations from the mean.
